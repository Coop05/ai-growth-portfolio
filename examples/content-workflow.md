# Example: meeting notes to a content draft

**Fictional portfolio illustration.** Input, brief and draft were created for this repository. They are not copied from a client engagement or a production run.

[Back to the case study](../case-studies/ai-workflow-system.md)

## Input

> In our fictional team's weekly review, we noticed that different people were starting the same research task with different instructions. We agreed to document the required input, the expected output and the review checklist. We have not measured the effect on turnaround time or quality yet.

## Writing brief

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | People introducing AI into recurring team tasks |
| Observation | Similar tasks were being started with different instructions |
| Point | Define what a useful output looks like before trying to repeat the workflow |
| Supported facts | The team agreed to document input, output and review requirements |
| Unknown | Whether this improved speed or quality |
| Exclude | Invented savings, customer results, claims of full autonomy |

## Reusable instruction

```text
Create a writing brief from the supplied notes before drafting.
List supported facts and unknowns separately.
Write one concise British English draft for the stated audience.
Do not invent metrics, examples, quotes or outcomes.
Keep the draft available for human review; do not publish it.
```

## Illustrative draft

Three people can use the same AI tool and still get three very different answers to the same task.

In this example, the team started by writing down three things: what goes in, what should come out and what a reviewer needs to check.

That gives the next person a place to start. It also gives the team a shared way to discuss an answer that misses the mark.

The next step is to test whether the workflow produces more useful outputs. Until then, a documented process is the result we can point to. Claims about time saved will need evidence.

## Review criteria

Check that the draft keeps the example fictional, preserves the distinction between an implemented process and a measured outcome, and makes a clear point without adding unsupported proof.
